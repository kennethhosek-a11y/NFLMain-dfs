# Predict the field's lineups, then count them

The best NFL DraftKings ownership projections are built in one of two ways, and the strongest shops increasingly combine them. In the first, a model or analyst estimates each player's %Drafted from salary, projection, value, Vegas, injury news and positional scarcity, then rescales each position so the totals add up. In the second, the provider simulates the whole field's lineups and reads ownership off them as a count (SaberSim says this outright; Stokastic, The Solver and the open-source tools consume ownership to build such fields). Accuracy is poorly documented. The only public anchors are vendor self-reports: about **3 points of average error per player** across all players, and ETR's **.923 r-squared** on the DraftKings flagship tournament against **.856** in small fields. The one peer-reviewed model (Haugh & Singal, *Management Science* 2021) shows exactly where models miss: they **under-predict the single chalkiest player**, they miss **"momentum" after a big prior week**, and they miss **late news**. Ownership is chalkier in single-entry and high-stakes contests than in the Milly Maker, chalkier on short slates, and only partly reacts to Sunday-morning inactives, because much of the field builds early and never edits. For Kenny's NFL Main system, the NBA design carries over in outline. The concrete changes are these. Set totals position by position, not just to 900% overall. Model the field's habits (FLEX choice, stacking) from DraftKings contest downloads. Use a simple model longer than four slates, because an NFL season has only about 17 main slates. Add a recency feature and a chalk-sharpening step. Damp late news by when it broke. Grade every snapshot against the contest type actually entered.

## Only one provider documents its method, and it counts simulated lineups

Most providers say almost nothing about how they project ownership. **SaberSim is the only vendor that documents a mechanism.** "Ownership is a descriptive statistic of our field lineups," and its model simulates "how entire fields of DFS opponents react to news, salaries, and projections" ([SaberSim: How Projections Work](https://support.sabersim.com/en/articles/12078831-how-projections-work)). It builds opponent fields from "industry-aggregated projections that reflect actual construction and ownership trends", for **13 contest types** and four stake buckets (≤$4, $4.01–$30, $30.01–$99.99, ≥$100). Its contest sims run on the field lineups themselves, not on the percentages ([SaberSim: How Contest Sims Work](https://support.sabersim.com/en/articles/12079199-how-contest-sims-work)). ETR describes only its outputs: separate large-field (flagship), medium-field and small-field DraftKings projections, "our team" as the author, and a feed into The Solver optimizer ([ETR DraftKings Projections](https://establishtherun.com/draftkings-projections/); [ETR large/medium/small field](https://establishtherun.com/draftkings-projected-ownership-large-medium-small-field/)). Stokastic publishes Tuesday night and updates "continuously" for injuries ([Stokastic NFL DFS Ownership](https://www.stokastic.com/nfl/nfl-dfs-ownership)). RotoGrinders now says its numbers are "built using advanced machine learning models", with no further detail ([RotoGrinders THE BLITZ](https://rotogrinders.com/the-blitz)). Historically the numbers were made by hand: Adam Levitan projected FantasyLabs ownership player by player in 2019 ([FantasyLabs 2019](https://www.fantasylabs.com/articles/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-draftkings-fanduel-week-10-2019/)), and Chris Gimino did RotoGrinders' in 2016 ([RotoGrinders Wk 4 2016](https://rotogrinders.com/articles/nfl-projected-ownership-analysis-week-4-1454863)).

The one fully published per-player method is 4for4's "implied ownership", and it looks a lot like Kenny's NBA baseline. 4for4 regresses first-place Milly Maker scores on salary to get a target score for each position. It then estimates each player's chance of reaching that target from his projection and spread, divides by the position's sum, and multiplies by an expected position total (RB **240.5%** in its worked example) ([4for4 GPP Leverage Scores](https://www.4for4.com/gpp-leverage-scores-balancing-value-ownership-dfs)). That is a share model with position totals, the same family as a softmax normalized to 800%. The difference is that 4for4 normalizes **within each position** rather than across the whole slate.

The only tested statistical model is academic. Haugh & Singal model each position's ownership as a **Dirichlet regression**: the expected shares inside a position are a softmax of a weighted sum of features, so they add up within the position automatically. The features were **salary, projected points and FantasyPros' ownership estimate**. Opponent lineups are then sampled position by position, with a "stacker" share that pairs the QB with his top WR, and a salary floor that matches the field's real budget use ([Haugh & Singal working paper](http://www.columbia.edu/~mh2078/DFS_Revision_1_May2019.pdf); [Management Science record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:67:y:2021:i:1:p:72-92)). Two of their findings matter directly for Kenny. First, treating ownership as fixed and certain "led to over-certainty and poor performance", so the model needs uncertainty built in. Second, **salary plus projection alone nearly matched the full model** in leave-one-week-out testing (QB log-likelihood 742.71 against 742.88), while the outside ownership estimate on its own was clearly worse (690.79). Most of the money came from having *any* ownership estimate at all. Their simulated top-heavy profit was about $1,400 without an opponent model and $5,400 with simple fixed ownership. The full uncertain model added only about 10% more, reaching $6,000 ([Haugh & Singal working paper](http://www.columbia.edu/~mh2078/DFS_Revision_1_May2019.pdf)). Gradient boosting, beta regression and neural nets do not appear in any public, validated NFL ownership work. Vendors mention "machine learning" but publish no specifications.

## Salary-value and injury news drive ownership; the field follows consensus projections

Every source puts the same drivers at the top: salary and points per dollar, consensus projections, injury-created value, Vegas totals and positional scarcity. Recency bias, name brand and touting come next ([RotoGrinders, Gimino, "Predicting Ownership"](https://rotogrinders.com/articles/dfs-strategy-predicting-ownership-1360237)). What is missing is measurement. **No public source gives effect sizes** for prior-week points, salary changes, wind, popular teams or Vegas elasticities. Kenny's system will have to fit them from its own data.

**Value is relative to the slate and to the position.** Ownership reflects "how obvious, or hidden, a value play looks next to everyone else on the slate" ([Fantasy Team Advice](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nfl/dfs-ownership-projections)). The field spends almost all its money (the 2022 Milly field averaged **$49,877 of $50,000**), so cheap value is what lets people afford studs ([ETR/Levitan 2023](https://establishtherun.com/levitan-how-to-win-draftkings-milly-maker-in-2023/)). Injury replacements are the biggest single source of extreme chalk, because DraftKings prices do not adjust for injuries ([Clemson study](https://news.clemson.edu/study-says-skill-strategies-exist-in-daily-fantasy-sports-games/)). In Week 1 of 2026, after Brock Bowers was hurt, backup TE Michael Mayer at **$2,900 was projected 25.1% owned**, fourth-highest on the slate. The top RB, Jahmyr Gibbs, was at 47–53% depending on the site and version ([Stokastic Week 1 2026](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/dfs-strategy/draftkings-nfl-dfs-cheat-sheet-week-1)).

**The field follows consensus, not sharp projections.** SaberSim builds its fields from industry-aggregated projections, not its own. ETR's three-season data show that field ownership tracks actual value much less well than ETR's projections do. For RBs, field ownership correlated **0.33–0.39** with salary-adjusted actual value, against **0.52–0.55** for ETR's projections. For WRs it was **0.22–0.30** against 0.41–0.44 ([ETR/Levitan 2023](https://establishtherun.com/levitan-how-to-win-draftkings-milly-maker-in-2023/)). The field is not dumb, though. Raw ownership correlated with actual DraftKings points about as well as salary did at each position (QB .506 against .556) ([ETR/Levitan](https://establishtherun.com/levitan-how-to-win-draftkings-nfl-milly-maker/)). For a model, this means ownership should be driven by **what the public sees**: DraftKings' own and free-site projections and value, not Kenny's sharper projection. A sharp edge in Kenny's projection is a reason to differ from the field, not a predictor of what the field will do.

Vegas and weather work mostly through the projection layer. High totals and big favorites draw ownership, and a game at the slate's second-lowest total (39.5) drew "incredibly low" projected ownership ([SI Week 9 2026](https://www.si.com/onsi/fantasy/dfs/brock-purdy-kyren-williams-headline-the-best-nfl-week-9-dfs-cash-game-plays)). Wind above roughly 15 mph is widely said to push the public off passing games, often too far ([WalterFootball](https://walterfootball.com/dfsweather.php)). Where options are thin (TE, and RB when it is worth spending up), ownership concentrates. Where they are deep (QB), it spreads. Typical top ownership on a 10–13 game main slate is asserted at **18–25% for the top QB** ([DFS Engineer 2026](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)), against 40–60% for an elite or injury-boosted RB.

## A 3-point average error hides the misses that matter: the chalk tail and news

No independent, multi-provider accuracy study of NFL ownership exists in public. What exists is self-graded. Fantasy Team Advice reports an average error of **3.0 points across 23,375 graded player projections**, measured as the projection shown at lock against real large-field tournament lineups. An earlier version of its page said 3.6 points across 31,479 projections ([Fantasy Team Advice](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nfl/dfs-ownership-projections); [FTA accuracy page](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nfl/dfs-ownership-accuracy)). ETR claims **.923 r-squared** against the DraftKings flagship since 2022 and **.856** for small fields. That figure comes from a summary of its sales page and was not confirmed ([ETR In-Season Package](https://establishtherun.com/in-season-package/)). In 2016, hand-made RotoGrinders projections correlated about **.89–.96** with actual ownership week to week ([RotoGrinders Wk 10 2016](https://rotogrinders.com/articles/nfl-projected-ownership-analysis-week-10-1541684)). FTN publishes weekly head-to-head r-squared and RMSE comparisons and names ETR as its closest rival, but the numbers could not be retrieved ([FTN ownership review](https://ftnfantasy.com/nfl/week-13-nfl-dfs-ownership-review)).

These headline numbers flatter everyone. Most of a main-slate pool is owned under 1%, so the average error over all players is pulled down by easy zeros. Correlation is high for any model that knows salary and news. **The errors that cost money are on the 15–60% players**, and that is where the evidence shows systematic misses. Haugh & Singal's intervals were well calibrated overall (95% intervals covered 93–97% of outcomes). But the most-picked player landed in an extreme percentile **12.5% of the time in top-heavy contests and 19.5% in double-ups**, against an expected 5%. The model under-predicted the chalkiest player. Their worst miss was Ezekiel Elliott at about **80% owned in a double-up when the model's 95% range was 0–10%**, after he had been a top RB for two straight weeks. They also missed Adrian Peterson after a 25-point game against a 7-point projection, and players whose news broke after the projection was set ([Haugh & Singal working paper](http://www.columbia.edu/~mh2078/DFS_Revision_1_May2019.pdf)). Their own conclusion: **momentum/recency features and late-news updates** would have helped most. Practitioners agree that "narrative-driven" spikes are the blind spot of algorithmic models, and that pre-inactive projections can miss backups who step into starting roles by 8–12 points. That claim is unmeasured ([DFS Engineer 2026](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)).

There is no published bucket-by-bucket calibration, position or salary-tier bias study, or season trend from any provider. One unmeasured vendor heuristic is worth borrowing: when ownership sources disagree by about 15 points on a player, treat that player's number as highly uncertain ([DFS Engineer 2026](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)). Realistic targets for a home-built model, inferred from the vendor claims rather than proven, are an all-player average error of **3 points or less at lock** and **4–6 points on players projected at 10% or more**.

## Ownership freezes early, is chalkier in small fields, and concentrates on short slates

**Late news is only partly absorbed, and the share depends on when it breaks.** Stokastic says "much of the NFL DFS field" sets a lineup Sunday morning and never touches it again. Late-game ownership "barely moves once the early games start", and "what the field committed at lineup lock is what it is running" ([Stokastic NFL late swap](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/nfl-dfs/nfl-dfs-late-swap)). Inactives arrive about 90 minutes before each kickoff (about 11:30 ET for the 1:00 games, 2:35 for the 4:05/4:25 games), and practitioners call them the largest late shift ([Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/nfl-dfs/nfl-dfs-late-swap); [DFS Engineer 2026](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)). Projection error is asserted to tighten from about ±8–10 points on Thursday to ±4–6 by Saturday night ([DFS Engineer 2026](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)). No one has published what share of the field builds before or after inactives. The consequence is a trap in both directions. A projection that assumes the whole field reacts will **over-project** a backup elevated at 11:30. A projection frozen before the news will **under-project** him. Kenny's NBA finding that the field moved only about 20% of the way on 11/04 is the same effect. But NFL news arrives in waves: Wednesday–Friday practice reports, Friday status designations, Saturday moves, Sunday-morning reports, and inactives at 11:30 and 2:35. The early waves are absorbed much more fully than the late ones. One damping number will not fit all of them.

**Contest type changes ownership a lot, and in a predictable direction.** SaberSim gives one player as **65% owned in a high-stakes single-entry against 41% in a large-field mass-entry contest**, and another as **40% in a low-stakes single-entry against 20% in a high-stakes large field** ([SaberSim: How Contest Sims Work](https://support.sabersim.com/en/articles/12079199-how-contest-sims-work)). One high-stakes $4,444 DraftKings contest had Alvin Kamara at nearly 70% ([Medium, Intelligent Sports Wagerer](https://medium.com/the-intelligent-sports-wagerer/draftkings-week-4-high-stakes-ownership-review-7bf8bc804039)). ETR's drop from .923 to .856 r-squared in small fields shows those fields are also harder to predict. That is why ETR, SaberSim and Fantasy Team Advice all say large-field projections "may miss in smaller or different contests" ([FTA accuracy page](https://fantasyteamadvice.com/nfl/dfs-ownership-accuracy)). Nobody publishes the size of the Milly vs 150-max vs single-entry gap on the same slate. DraftKings shows %Drafted for every contest after lock, so Kenny can measure it himself.

**Fewer games concentrate ownership.** Top QB ownership is asserted at about 18–25% on 10–13 game main slates and 30–40% on 3–4 game slates ([DFS Engineer 2026](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)). On Thanksgiving, even normally flat positions "could approach 50% ownership in even the largest fields" ([4for4 Thanksgiving 2023](https://www.4for4.com/2023/w12/fanduel-draftkings-nfl-dfs-thanksgiving-slate-breakdown)). Main slates themselves vary from about 8 to 14 games across bye weeks, so slate size should be a model input, not a constant.

## Duplicates and stacks come from the field's joint lineups, not from player percentages

Player percentages do not say which players appear *together*, and in NFL that matters. Stokastic shows a QB projected at 11.5% owned who heads the field's stacks 21.5% of the time ([Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/nfl-dfs/how-to-build-nfl-dfs-lineups)). The common duplicate formula, other copies = (entries − 1) × product of each player's ownership, assumes players are picked independently. Stokastic notes that this is reasonable for NBA but breaks for NFL and MLB stacks ([Stokastic NBA guide](https://www.stokastic.com/articles/nba-dfs/nba-dfs-strategy-guide); [dfs-dupe](https://github.com/wjwhitmire/dfs-dupe)). In NFL Showdown, product ownership was still the best single predictor of duplicate count, but it explained only **r² = 0.43** ([ETR Showdown 101](https://establishtherun.com/nfl-showdown-101/)).

The measured field habits come from ETR's Milly studies (roughly 2020–2022). About **80–90% of lineups stack**. About **12–13% double-stack** the QB with two pass catchers, about **4% triple-stack**, and about **35% add a bring-back** from the opponent. The field puts a **WR in the FLEX about 48% of the time** and a TE there 12–19% of the time ([ETR/Levitan 2022](https://establishtherun.com/levitan-how-to-win-draftkings-milly-maker-in-2022/); [ETR/Levitan 2023](https://establishtherun.com/levitan-how-to-win-draftkings-milly-maker-in-2023/); [Fantasy Footballers Milly trends](https://www.thefantasyfootballers.com/dfs/dfs-milly-maker-trends-how-to-win-big-on-draftkings-fantasy-football/)). Haugh & Singal's stricter definition (QB with his top WR only) gave 20–35% stackers on 2016–17 FanDuel. Their field also used **99.5% of salary on average** ([Haugh & Singal working paper](http://www.columbia.edu/~mh2078/DFS_Revision_1_May2019.pdf)). The FLEX split also fixes the position totals on DraftKings Classic. QB and DST are 100% each, and RB + WR + TE = 700%, split roughly RB 235–240%, WR 345–350% and TE 112–119% by those FLEX shares. 4for4's 240.5% RB median fits this ([4for4](https://www.4for4.com/gpp-leverage-scores-balancing-value-ownership-dfs)).

There are two practical ways to simulate the field. The first samples lineups by ownership under structure rules. The open-source chanzer0 NFL-DFS-Tools defaults to 65% of lineups stacked (40% of those QB+2), a $49,200 salary floor, and a projection floor within 25% of optimal ([chanzer0/NFL-DFS-Tools](https://github.com/chanzer0/NFL-DFS-Tools)). The second re-runs an optimizer many times on noisy consensus projections and counts how often each player appears ([Advanced Sports Analytics](https://advancedsportsanalytics.substack.com/p/getting-ready-for-nfl-simulations)). Neither publishes calibration against real DraftKings fields. The first has a known flaw: the salary and stacking rules distort the ownership it was given, inflating cheap chalk and stacked pass catchers. It needs a re-weighting loop that nudges each player's sampling weight until the simulated exposure matches the target. **DraftKings' contest download ("Export Lineups to CSV") contains every entry's full lineup plus each player's %Drafted. It is available for only 10 days after the contest** ([DK help](https://z3n-sq-gaming.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/18427755136791-How-do-I-download-a-CSV-to-see-GameCenter-standings-for-a-contest-US)). It is the only source for current stack rates, FLEX splits, salary use and true duplicate counts, so it has to be saved every week or it is lost.

## What Kenny's NFL Main system should do

The NBA design (a hand-tuned share baseline, a learned model that must beat it on held-out slates, damped late news, and graded snapshots) is sound and matches what the field-modeling literature supports. The table adapts it to NFL. Evidence grades: **Strong** means tested in the academic model or measured from contest data. **Moderate** means consistent across several vendors or practitioners, or measured once. **Weak** means it follows from the findings but is untested, so it is a starting guess to be checked against Kenny's own results.

| 14 | **Add a wind flag** (forecast above about 15 mph) that lowers that game's QB/WR/TE ownership. Fit the size from data. | Widely agreed direction, no measured size. | Weak ([WalterFootball](https://walterfootball.com/dfsweather.php)) |
| 15 | **Show an uncertainty flag** on players whose role depends on a teammate's status, or where outside sources disagree by about 15 points, so the builder doesn't lean hard on a fade or a pivot that rests on a shaky number. | Errors concentrate on news-dependent players. | Weak ([DFS Engineer](https://www.dfsengineer.com/blog/nfl-dfs-ownership-projections-full-leverage-guide-2026/)) |

Two cautions apply. First, the starting numbers in items 2, 8 and 13 come from 2020–2022 Milly studies and one-off vendor claims. The field's habits drift, so each should be overwritten by Kenny's own downloads as soon as there are four to six weeks of them. Second, as in NBA, a projection that does not beat the baseline on held-out weeks should not take over, however sophisticated it is. In the academic test, most of the value came from having *any* reasonable ownership number, and modest gains came from refinement.

## Conclusion

The real lesson from the providers is that ownership is a **by-product of a model of the field**, not a stand-alone forecast. The best shops model how the public builds lineups (which projections it reads, how it stacks, how much salary it spends, when it stops editing), and the percentages fall out. That is why SaberSim, Stokastic and The Solver can serve separate numbers by contest type and react to news within minutes. Kenny's NBA system already has the right skeleton. Turning it toward NFL means moving the main effort from tuning per-player weights to recording and copying the field's lineup habits, using the full lineups in DraftKings' contest downloads.

The thin public record is an opportunity as much as a gap. No provider publishes calibration by ownership level, contest-type gaps, or how much of a Sunday-morning reaction the field actually makes. A system that saves every snapshot and every contest download will, within one season, hold data on its own contests that no public source offers. The weakest assumptions it starts with (the late-news damping by time bucket, the FLEX split, the stack rate) are exactly the numbers that data will settle first.
